How to stop doomscrolling: 9 fixes that actually work

Updated 20 November 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: you stop doomscrolling by breaking the loop, not by trying harder. Make the apps slower to open, remove the moments that trigger a scroll (bed, waking up, waiting), and give yourself something to do instead. The fixes that stick add real friction between the urge and the feed, like having to walk before an app unlocks.

Why doomscrolling is so hard to stop

Doomscrolling is scrolling long past the point where it feels good, often through bad news or content that leaves you anxious, numb or behind. Almost everyone does it, and it's not a character flaw. Feeds are built so there's always one more post, and you never know when the next good one will appear. That unpredictability is exactly what keeps you pulling.

It also runs on autopilot. Most scrolls don't start with a decision. They start with a cue: you wake up, you sit down on the bus, you feel bored or stressed, and your thumb opens the app before you've thought about it. That's why "just use it less" rarely works. Willpower acts after the app is already open. The fixes below work earlier, on the cue and on how easy the app is to open.

9 fixes, easiest first

1. Find your trigger times

Open Settings › Screen Time › See All App & Website Activity on your iPhone and look at when and where the hours go. Most people find two or three hotspots: first thing in the morning, late at night in bed, and dead time like commuting or waiting. You don't need to fix your whole day, just those moments.

2. Move the apps off your home screen

If TikTok or Instagram sits where your thumb lands, opening it is a reflex. Move them into a folder on your last screen, or remove them from the home screen entirely and open them through search. Every extra second between the urge and the feed gives you a chance to change your mind.

3. Turn off the notifications that pull you in

Likes, "you might have missed" alerts and live notifications exist to bring you back. In Settings › Notifications, switch them off for social and news apps. Keep messages from real people if you need them.

4. Charge your phone outside the bedroom

Late-night scrolling is the most common doomscroll of all, and it eats into sleep too. Charge your phone in another room, or at least across the room, and use a cheap alarm clock. If that's too big a step, set Downtime in Screen Time for your bedtime hours.

5. Don't start the day in the feed

Whatever you see first sets the tone. Make a rule that the first 30 minutes after waking are feed-free: get dressed, drink water, go outside. A short walk in the morning works well because it replaces the scroll with something that actually wakes you up.

6. Use Screen Time limits, and know their weak spot

iPhone's built-in App Limits can cap social apps at, say, 30 minutes a day. They help, but they have a well-known weak spot: when you hit the limit, you can tap "Ignore Limit" and keep going. 8. Replace the scroll, don't just ban it

Scrolling fills a need: a break, a distraction, company. If you only take it away, the urge comes back louder. Pick a replacement for each trigger time: a podcast on the commute, a book by the bed, a 10-minute walk when you feel stuck at your desk. The replacement should be just as easy to start as opening the app.

9. Do it with someone

Habits are easier to keep when someone else is watching. Tell a friend what you're trying, or set a shared goal. A simple 7-day plan

  1. Day 1: check Screen Time and write down your two worst trigger times.
  2. Day 2: move social apps off your home screen and turn off their notifications.
  3. Day 3: charge your phone outside the bedroom tonight.
  4. Day 4: no feed for the first 30 minutes after waking. Take a short walk instead.
  5. Day 5: add friction to your worst app, with an App Limit or a step goal that has to be walked first.
  6. Day 6: pick one replacement activity for each trigger time.
  7. Day 7: compare this week's Screen Time with last week's and keep what worked.

You won't go from hours a day to zero, and you don't need to. The goal is to scroll because you chose to, not because your thumb got there first.

FAQ

What is doomscrolling?

Doomscrolling is scrolling through feeds or news for far longer than you meant to, usually through content that makes you feel worse. The word took off around 2020 and now covers any compulsive, mindless scrolling.

Why can't I stop doomscrolling even when I want to?

Because it runs on habit, not decisions. A cue like boredom or lying in bed triggers the scroll automatically, and endless feeds never give you a natural stopping point. That's why changing your environment works better than relying on willpower.

Do screen time limits actually work?

They help as a reminder, but iPhone's App Limits can be dismissed with "Ignore Limit", so on their own they're easy to skip. They work better combined with friction you can't dismiss, like moving apps off the home screen or having to walk before an app unlocks.
